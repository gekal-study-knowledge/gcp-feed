<div class="block-paragraph_advanced"><p><span style="vertical-align: baseline;">There’s a lot more to AI infrastructure than just buying accelerators. Indeed, the infrastructure mandate for enterprise AI has shifted from acquiring accelerators to keeping them fully utilized. Next to these expensive accelerators, storage accounts for only a small sliver of total AI spend. However, having the wrong storage in place can put the rest of your committed compute capital at risk.</span></p>
<p><span style="vertical-align: baseline;">To optimize your accelerators’ TCO, you need to keep them fully occupied. Very often, the challenge here is data I/O. If accelerators sit waiting to read or write data, you’re wasting money. Across the industry, insufficient storage throughput has become a primary impediment to AI success, starving models of the data they need. And the problem is only going to get worse. Gartner </span><a href="https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-04-28-gartner-identifies-six-steps-to-manage-artificial-intelligence-agent-sprawl" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><span style="text-decoration: underline; vertical-align: baseline;">predicts</span></a><span style="vertical-align: baseline;"> enterprises will deploy over 150,000 agents in the next few years, so the I/O bottleneck and the challenge of orchestrating storage for agentic fleets will only compound. </span></p>
<p><span style="vertical-align: baseline;">Organizations are eager to move their AI initiatives forward, but preventing idle compute requires a purpose-built approach to data I/O. With the demands of AI, standard enterprise storage — even a system optimized for fast analytics — will inevitably suffer from bottlenecks that leave compute resources twiddling their proverbial thumbs.</span></p>
<h3><strong style="vertical-align: baseline;">AI is not a monolithic workload</strong></h3>
<p><span style="vertical-align: baseline;">The most common architectural misstep we see is treating AI as a single, general-purpose workload. In reality, the physical movement of data transforms across the AI lifecycle, creating distinct I/O profiles based on how models consume and generate information:</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="vertical-align: baseline;">
<p><span style="vertical-align: baseline;">In </span><strong style="vertical-align: baseline;">model training</strong><span style="vertical-align: baseline;">, clusters read datasets at high throughput, but pause regularly to write checkpoints to record their state in event of a failure. When thousands of GPUs attempt to save this data in a single, simultaneous burst, standard storage architectures stall. That’s especially true of traditional cloud storage, which is engineered for durability. Writing checkpoints triggers thousands of administrative database transactions to safely record the files, bogging down the system, choking the network, and freezing the entire GPU cluster until the save is complete. Furthermore, saving the data is only one of the problems: If a server fails, slow storage turns a routine checkpoint recovery into a multi-hour delay, leaving expensive accelerators sitting completely idle.</span></p>
</li>
<li style="vertical-align: baseline;">
<p><strong style="vertical-align: baseline;">Inference</strong><span style="vertical-align: baseline;">, conversely, is real-time and latency-sensitive. As AI services scale to meet user demand, inference clusters constantly load and unload petabyte-scale model repositories quickly. In this case, a slow storage system can leave GPUs in a “cold start” as they wait for data.  Also, many models now process context windows that exceed 100,000 tokens, filling up accelerator memory. Without a high-speed external cache to offload this context, when a user asks the next question, the accelerator must recalculate previous inputs, wasting compute on redundant operations.</span></p>
</li>
</ul>
